each cap buys" and the 3.1%, 3.6%, 3.8% and 5.8% quoted under "Reading it"

The "effective names" figures are also replaced by that rerun. They were
1 / sum of squared weights, which counts idle cash as names: a median
seven-name book at a 5% cap read as 57. The scorecard now uses
(sum w)² / sum w² over the invested weight, which gives 7 for that book.

The CAGR and drawdown figures are unaffected: they come from the daily

now also measures drawdown from the starting NAV. That can change a figure

The `/4` candidate holds every A/A+ member of the point-in-time book at

## Reading it

The median session of the choosing window has seven A/A+ names in the
point-in-time book, so the largest weight is 14.3% on a median day at
every cap of 15% or more: the cap is not what sizes the book on an
ordinary day. It binds on the days when only one, two or three names
qualify, and what it does then is leave cash idle: 60% of the book at a
20% cap when two names qualify, 90% at a 5% cap when two qualify. That
idle cash is the whole cost of the cap. From 5% to 20% it is nineteen
CAGR points; from 20% to 25% it is 1.2 points; from 25% to 33% it is 0.2;
above 33% the cap never binds with three or more names and there is
nothing left to buy or pay.

What the cap buys is a bound on one name. At 20% the worst single-name
day in eight years cost 3.1% of the book; at 25%, 3.6%; at 33%, 3.8%; with
no cap a name held at half the book lost 11.5% in a day, 5.8% of the book.
On 2024-2026, with five to six effective names on a median day, the
largest weight is 20% at every cap of 20% or more: the recent book is
already at the limit on an ordinary day, which is why the 20% and 25%
lines separate there (46.2% against 48.5%).

The multiplicity: eight caps were scored, none of the differences between
20%, 25% and 33% is distinguishable from equal weight or from each other
(t 0.2 to 0.9 against the hurdle; the paired difference between adjacent
caps is smaller still), and the ordering above 20% is monotone in the
direction the mechanics predict rather than a fitted optimum. The cost
curve is the finding, not a winner.

## What this means for the choice

The 20% cap is not random - it is the knee of the cost curve, the last
point before the cap stops costing more than a point a year - but it is
the conservative end of a flat region. The honest statement of the
trade-off the operator is making is this: moving the limit from 20% to
25% buys about a point a year on the choosing window and two on
2024-2026, and pays for it with a 25% largest position instead of 20% and
a worst single-name day of 3.6% instead of 3.1%; moving to 33% adds a
further two tenths and pays 33% and 3.8%. Beyond 33% there is nothing to
buy. Anything at or below 15% pays for concentration control with idle
cash at a rate no selection signal in this repository has ever earned
back.

The candidate stays at 20% for the fidelity shadow, because the shadow
measures execution and any cap in the flat region measures it equally
well, and because changing the candidate's parameters after seeing its
score is the thing the gate exists to prevent. The limit is the operator's
to move, with this note as the price list; if it moves, it moves before
the shadow's verdict is read, not after.

An alternative worth a registered trial later, with no cap at all: when
fewer names qualify than the cap allows, hold the rest of the book at
equal weight instead of cash. It removes the parameter and the idle cash
at once; what it costs is that on the days the grade says "few good
names" the book ignores it. That is a different claim and gets its own
arm.
